Chido

A self-order kiosk experience for a Mexican street food restaurant: photo-led, quick to tap through, and full of personality.

00Overview

The brief

Chido is a Mexican street food restaurant, and the kiosk is the first thing a hungry guest touches. I designed the full ordering flow for a portrait touchscreen: from the welcome screen, through dine in or take away, to browsing the menu and customizing a dish, all in Hebrew and right to left, and all in Chido's loud, sunny brand.

01Problem

Understood in seconds, with no one to explain it

Problem

The counter is slow

Ordering at a busy counter is slow, and a long menu with sauces and extras is easy to get wrong. A kiosk has to be understood in seconds by a first time guest, with no staff to explain it, while still feeling like the restaurant and not like a generic checkout machine.

Solution

A short, photo first flow

A full screen welcome, one big choice between dine in and take away, a menu built around appetizing food photography, and a dish page where sauces and extras are picked by tapping pictures instead of reading options.

Approach

Grayscale first, brand second

I started from grayscale wireframes to settle the structure and the tap order, then dressed them in the brand: warm paper textures, yellow outlined photo frames, and a corn mascot who shows up at the moments a guest needs a friendly nudge.

02Structure

One main screen, six ways in

Before drawing a single screen, I mapped the whole kiosk as a sitemap. A guest enters through the welcome screen, chooses dine in or take away, and lands on a main screen that fans out into six categories: starters, tacos, burritos, Con Amigos deals, desserts, and drinks. Everything else in the product hangs off that one screen.

Simple items

Straight to the dish

Starters, desserts and drinks are single taps, with a sauce choice only where the dish comes with one.

Tacos & burritos

Split in two

Ready made options, or a build your own path through tortilla, protein, topping, sauce, and a side dip, one choice per step.

Deals

Guided bundles

Con Amigos bundles for three or six friends walk a guest through tacos, burritos and drinks, and a "Fiesta" option routes big groups to the restaurant.

Checkout

One path for everything

Cart, a suggestion pop up, order summary with editing, name and phone, payment method, and the order confirmation.

03Scenario

Testing the longest path: a custom taco

To test the longest path through the kiosk, I wrote out a scenario for a guest who builds their own taco. It starts with a tap to order, then take away, taco, and custom taco, followed by five small choices: tortilla, protein, topping, sauce and side dip. The cart then offers a pop up with an extra, in this case nachos, before the order summary, name and phone, payment, and the confirmation.

04Wireframes

Hierarchy before color

Before any color or photography, I built the flow as low fidelity wireframes on the same 1080 × 1920 canvas. That kept the conversation about hierarchy: what a guest sees first, where the thumb goes next, and where the order always lives.

One decision per screen

Dine in or take away gets its own screen with two large buttons, so nothing competes for attention.

A menu with rhythm

One wide featured card on top, a two column grid below, and a category rail down the side to jump between sections.

An order bar that stays

A persistent bar at the bottom holds the running order, a cancel link, and a single green button to pay.

Dishes ask small questions

Sauces and extras sit under the dish photo as tappable picture tiles, with quantity and add to order pinned at the bottom.

05Screens

From welcome to dish, in five steps

The final screens keep the wireframe structure and add the brand on top. The flow reads in five steps, from a guest walking up to the kiosk to a dish in the order.

06Decisions

Order with your eyes first

Photography

Food photography leads

Every item is a large, yellow outlined photo with its name and price underneath, so a guest orders with their eyes first and reads second.

Buttons

Big and unmistakable

Dine in and take away are two full width green buttons with the mascot at a table on one and walking off with a bag on the other.

Direction

Right to left from the ground up

The layout, category rail, prices and order bar are mirrored for Hebrew, while the English tagline stays in the brand's display type.

Customizing

Pictures, not option lists

Sauces are picked from small photo tiles, and extras like cheddar are a single tap, so customizing a dish never becomes a form.

Language

A switch within reach

A small globe sits in the corner of the entry screens for guests who prefer another language.

Total

Always visible

The order bar and the dish page bar keep price, quantity and the next action in the same place on every screen.

07Brand

A street food counter, not a template

The identity is retro and playful: a chunky, rounded wordmark in sunshine yellow with a deep green shadow, a script tagline, and a corn mascot in sunglasses who appears in several poses across the interface. Warm paper texture sits behind the menu, and the food photography leans on green tile and gingham cloth.

The poster

The identity also works in a single ink. This poster strips everything back to one green on warm paper: the wordmark, the script tagline arched over a walking mascot with a drink, halftone bands top and bottom, and small print lines like "Made fresh daily" and "Good vibes only" to set the tone.

08Kiosk

At real size

To check how the flow feels at real size, I placed the screens on freestanding ordering kiosks: a tall portrait display, with the card reader and receipt printer underneath. Side by side, the welcome, menu, and dish screens read as one continuous flow.

09Outcome

A first impression, not a form

A kiosk is a first impression

The same order can feel like a chore or like the start of a meal, and the brand, the photography, and the mascot are what set the tone.

Design for the thumb and the queue

Big targets, one decision at a time, and a total that never disappears keep the flow fast for someone standing up with people waiting behind them.

Wireframes earn their keep

Settling the structure in grayscale first let the brand and the photography go on top without reopening the layout.
